Audio Lesson “History of Colombia”

Introduction

Welcome to the audio lesson on the history of Colombia! Today we will make a journey through time—from the first settlements on the territory of the country to the modern searches for peace. This lesson will help you understand the key events that determined Colombia’s historical path. We will move sequentially through eleven topics that cover more than twelve thousand years of history.

Part 1. “Before Colombia”: the first settlements

Let us begin from the very beginning. Scholars still have not established exactly when and how the first people arrived on the territory of modern Colombia. However, specialists agree that around approximately the year 12,000 BCE men and women began to settle this territory. They moved along two possible routes: through the Darién Gap and along the coast of the Pacific Ocean.

The first settlers were hunter-gatherers. They led a nomadic way of life and moved following the animals they hunted. Evidence of their presence has been found in the caves of El Abra and Tequendama, in Tibitó on the Bogotá savanna, and also in places such as Chiribiquete in the Amazon valley. Archaeologists find stone flakes, animal bones, needles made from thorns and rodent bones.

It is important to note that, despite their nomadic way of life, these people were already familiar with agricultural techniques. Over time, as the population grew, some groups began to settle in permanent places. On the Atlantic coast, collective dwellings—malocas—have been found, in particular in Monsú, San Jacinto, and Puerto Hormiga. There, large accumulations of shells and ceramics have been discovered.

Between the year 3000 BCE and the beginning of the Common Era, nomadic groups began to transition to settled life. This was accompanied by social division of labor, the domestication of animals—for example, the guinea pig and the turkey—and also the emergence of exchange between neighbors. One of the most important results was the spread of maize, which came from Central America. Its traces have been found in Calima and on the Bogotá savanna. Cassava, which originated in Amazonia, also spread.

Maize became the most important food product, contributing to the transition to a settled way of life and the formation of the first complex societies—hierarchical organizations with division of labor and engineering achievements.

In the period from approximately the year 500 BCE to the year 1500 CE, the territory of modern Colombia became a space of significant transformations. Pottery and jewelry metalworking developed. A special place was occupied by the cultures of Quimbaya, Tairona, Tolima, Sinú, Chibcha, Guane, Tierradentro, Tumaco-La Tolita, and others. Works of these cultures are today exhibited in the museums of the Bank of the Republic throughout the country.

Technological achievements were accompanied by the emergence of cacicazgos—domains of chiefs—and confederations. The textbook highlights the six largest population groups: the Muisca, the Tairona, the Sinú, the Quimbaya and other peoples of the Cauca Valley, the Caribs, and also the peoples of Amazonia and the Orinoquía. Among the main language families, Chibchan, Arawakan, and Cariban predominated.

Part 2. The arrival of the Spaniards and New Granada

Now let us move to Europe. In the period from 500 to 1492, the European kingdoms were experiencing a crisis: a shortage of agricultural land and the exhaustion of production systems. The exception was the Italian city-states—Genoa, Rome, and Venice. On the Iberian Peninsula, living conditions were more difficult, so the Portuguese and Spaniards began maritime expeditions in search of new trade routes to the East.

In this era, the Italian navigator Christopher Columbus presented to Queen Isabella of Castile a proposal to finance an expedition. After approval, Columbus set out from Palos de la Frontera with three caravels. After several weeks of sailing, he reached the island of Guanahaní, passed along part of the eastern coast of Cuba and the island on which the modern Dominican Republic is located. Between 1492 and 1502 he made three more voyages, but died without ever learning that he had reached a new continent.

The first expeditions were not always distinguished by extreme cruelty. However, the need to recoup expenses and the desire to obtain rich plunder led the Spaniards increasingly to use violence. These abuses were condemned by the monk Bartolomé de las Casas.

To establish dominion, the Spaniards created governorates and applied the so-called requerimiento—a text that was read aloud to the indigenous inhabitants demanding that they accept the Christian faith and the authority of the Spanish king. Refusal was used as a justification for killings, torture, and enslavement.

By the time of the Spaniards’ arrival, about 8 million people lived on the territory of modern Colombia. Many were dispersed in small groups, but a significant part lived on the Bogotá savanna and in the south of the modern departments of Cauca and Nariño.

From 1535, campaigns into the interior regions began to be organized. In 1536, Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada set out with 800 people in search of a route to Peru and lands of emeralds. After almost a year of travel, about 180 people reached the lands of Vélez. The initial encounter escalated into open war, which ended in favor of the Spaniards. They founded Bogotá on August 6, 1538.

In this same area, three conquistadors met: Jiménez de Quesada, Sebastián de Belalcázar, and Nikolaus Federmann. All three claimed rights to the lands of the Muisca, so they traveled to Spain to settle their claims.

Wars and diseases led to a sharp reduction in the indigenous population—to less than 2 million people. The Crown prohibited the enslavement of indigenous inhabitants. The Laws of the Indies were created. However, the improvement in the legal status of indigenous inhabitants did not extend to the population of African origin, which was imported to replace local labor.

In the period from the 16th to the 18th century, Spain achieved a high level of development. Supplies of gold and silver from the New World played a significant role. However, royal officials did not always act honestly—the costly bureaucracy often redirected state revenues into personal pockets. This factor played a noticeable role in the transition to independence.

Part 3. The crisis of the monarchy and the movement for independence (1781–1810)

At the end of the 17th century and throughout the 18th century, Spain faced attacks by English pirates, separatist movements, and wars. To restore resources, Spain created the Viceroyalty of New Granada in 1719; in practice it began functioning in 1739.

The reforms were aimed at developing science, mining, and agriculture. One of the most important projects was the Botanical Expedition, associated with Álvaro Mutis. It gave Creoles—children of Spaniards born in America—the opportunity to participate in scientific activity. Among the well-known Creoles are Francisco José de Caldas and Antonio Nariño.

Tax reforms and increased control forced many Creoles to rethink their relations with the mother country. To this was added an interest in French ideas about “the power of the people” and liberalism.

The first revolutionary surge arose in 1779, when inspector Francisco Gutiérrez de Piñeres carried out a reorganization of tax revenues and raised prices. In 1781 a popular uprising broke out. The rebels came to be called comuneros. After negotiations with Archbishop Antonio Caballero y Góngora, agreements were reached: to abolish certain taxes and the tobacco monopoly. However, royal authority refused to recognize these agreements. José Antonio Galán led the continuation of the resistance, but his forces were defeated. Galán was sentenced to death.

In 1794, Antonio Nariño translated and published the “Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen.” Then the Napoleonic invasion of the Iberian Peninsula and the captivity of King Ferdinand VII plunged Spain into crisis. Part of the elite recognized Napoleon, while another part created local juntas, claiming to rule in the name of Ferdinand VII.

The inhabitants of New Granada began to create their own local juntas. One of the well-known documents was the “Memorial of Grievances,” written by Camilo Torres. Juntas appeared in many cities: Cartagena, Cali, Pamplona, Socorro, Bogotá, and others. Some remained loyal to the king, others sought autonomy, and still others advocated independence.

Differences between the provinces became the basis of the struggle in the period known as “Patria Boba” (1810–1814). These years were characterized by war between the provinces.

In Europe, England defeated Napoleon in 1814, and Ferdinand VII restored control over Spain. He sent to America an expedition numbering about 12,000 people under the command of Pablo Morillo. By 1815, Morillo had restored control over Caracas, was besieging Cartagena, and continued the offensive toward Bogotá. Many leaders of the movement were publicly executed, including Camilo Torres, Liborio Mejía, Joaquín Camacho, and Jorge Tadeo Lozano. Francisco José de Caldas and Policarpa Salavarrieta also died.

For the common population, the return of Spanish troops did not necessarily cause indignation—many better understood the familiar rule of the king. The middle and upper strata reacted differently: they rejected the army’s abuses and executions and increasingly supported the movement for independence.

Part 4. From Gran Colombia to New Granada (1819–1850)

The military success of the Spanish Reconquest forced the armies of the independence supporters to retreat to the Eastern Plains. This situation was used by Simón Bolívar, who in 1818 united with Francisco de Paula Santander. The key victories were the battle at Pantano de Vargas in July and the battle at Boyacá Bridge on August 7, 1819.

In December 1819, Bolívar convened the Congress of Angostura, which marked the beginning of the Republic of Colombia, which included the territories of modern Colombia, Panama, Venezuela, and Ecuador. Bolívar was elected president.

However, the young state faced sharp disputes: who should be considered a citizen, whether slaves should be freed, and what the form of the state should be. After the liberation campaigns in the south, Bolívar returned to Bogotá and in 1828 established a dictatorial regime. This led to conflict with Santander and an attempt on Bolívar’s life. He was saved thanks to Manuela Sáenz.

Seeing the direction in which the republic was developing, Bolívar decided to leave the country. He died in Santa Marta on December 17, 1830, poor and convinced that America could not be governed.

After the collapse of Gran Colombia, in 1832 the Republic of New Granada was created, including the territories of modern Colombia and Panama. During Santander’s rule in 1832–1837, important administrative reforms were carried out. After him, José Ignacio Márquez became president. He ordered the closure of small monasteries in order to use their buildings for schools. This caused the first major armed uprising—“The War of the Supreme”—which ended in 1841.

In 1843 the Constitution was changed: the right to vote was restricted to property owners and wealthy men over 25 years of age. In 1845, Tomás Cipriano de Mosquera became president. His government carried out a modernization program: measures and weights were standardized, the tobacco monopoly was abolished, and navigation on the Magdalena River was encouraged.

Part 5. The Liberal Revolution of the mid-19th century (1850–1880)

By the mid-19th century, one of society’s main disappointments was that the promises of independence had not been fully realized. In this period, the Conservative and Liberal parties emerged. The Conservatives believed that the Church should retain a leading role in education. The Liberals advocated the separation of Church and state.
